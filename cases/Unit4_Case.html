Valcortese Dairy: The Recall Weekend

A new CEO in Verona has 72 hours to handle a product recall — and a problem no recall will fix

Teaching case
Most leadership failures in a crisis come from solving the wrong kind of problem quickly and confidently.Core message · Unit 4 · ILO 1, 4

At 18:40 on a Friday, the quality laboratory of Valcortese Dairy, a family-owned cheese producer near Verona with 420 employees, confirmed Listeria monocytogenes in a batch of soft cheese. About 18,000 units from the batch had already been delivered to supermarkets in Italy, Austria and southern Germany. Marco Bellini, appointed CEO five months earlier after a career in management consulting, was called out of a dinner with the largest retail customer.

The technical side of the crisis was clear. The batch had to be recalled, the line cleaned and validated, the authorities informed and the retailers supplied with product information. The recall would cost an estimated €1.2 million. Two consumers in Austria had reported illness after eating the cheese; neither case had been confirmed as linked.

On Saturday morning the plant manager brought Marco a second story. The plant's AI-based quality monitoring system had flagged a temperature anomaly on cooling line 3 nine days earlier. The night-shift supervisor had overridden the alert. This was not unusual: the system had produced 63 alerts the previous month, 61 of them false, and supervisors had learned to treat it as 'the sensor that cries wolf'. Production targets had been raised by 8% that quarter, and stopping a line for an alert meant missing the shift target. Two of the overridden alerts had been real.

By late morning three proposals were on Marco's desk. The head of production wanted to replace the sensors, retrain the system and dismiss the night supervisor. The chair of the board, the founder's daughter, wanted a short statement calling the recall 'purely precautionary' with 'no confirmed link to illness'. The communications agency had used an AI tool to draft three statements, ranging from minimal to a full apology. The retailer wanted a public statement by Saturday at 12:00.

Marco suspects that the sensors are not the real problem. The supervisors trusted their experience over a system that had embarrassed them repeatedly; management had rewarded output over caution; and nobody had ever asked the shift teams what the alerts meant to them. Fixing that would require supervisors, production management and the board to give up habits and priorities that had served them well for decades. Marco now has to decide what to say, what to fix, and what to start.

Your tasks

  1. Separate the technical problems from the adaptive challenge. What does each require, and who must do the work?
  2. In which Cynefin domain is Marco on Friday night, and in which on Monday morning? How should his way of deciding change?
  3. Using SCCT, to which crisis cluster does the recall belong, and which response strategy follows? Evaluate the board chair's proposed statement.
  4. Why did the supervisors override the AI alerts? Use Levels of Automation and algorithm aversion to explain it, and propose a better design of human and AI roles.
  5. Decide: write Marco's plan for the first 72 hours — the public statement, two technical actions and one adaptive intervention.
