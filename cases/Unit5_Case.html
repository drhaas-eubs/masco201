Vireon Diagnostics: The Launch That Could Not Wait

A product lead in Milan must decide whether to challenge her COO two days before a board meeting

Teaching case
Knowing what is right is common; saying it to someone with power over you is the leadership skill.Core message · Unit 5 · ILO 1, 4

Vireon Diagnostics is a 420-person diagnostics company headquartered in Milan, with laboratories in Milan and Lyon. Its growth plan rests on TriageAssist, a software module that uses machine learning to rank incoming blood-panel results by urgency, so that laboratory physicians see the most critical cases first. A regional hospital network in Lombardy has signed a pilot contract worth €3.8 million over three years, on condition that TriageAssist goes live on 1 March.

Giulia Ferraro, 34, leads the TriageAssist product team. She reports to the Chief Operating Officer, Marco Bellandi, who has championed the product to the board and whose annual bonus is linked to the launch date. Ferraro admires Bellandi; he promoted her eighteen months ago.

Three weeks before launch, Ferraro's data scientist, Tomasz Wróbel, shows her an analysis from the final validation run. Overall, TriageAssist performs well: it ranks 96% of critical results in the top priority band. But for patients over 80, a group under-represented in the training data, only 88% of critical results reach the top band. In the pilot hospitals, roughly one result in nine from this age group comes from a patient over 80.

Wróbel believes that retraining with additional data from the Lyon laboratory would close most of the gap, but it would take about seven weeks, including revalidation. Ferraro also knows that software of this kind is likely to be treated as a high-risk AI system under the EU AI Act, which brings obligations for risk management, data governance and human oversight.

When Ferraro mentions the finding informally, Bellandi's reaction is quick: 'Ninety-six per cent is better than the current manual process. Physicians still review every result. If we slip, the hospital will sign with a competitor, and we lose the contract and possibly twenty jobs in Lyon. Document it and we fix it in version 1.1.' He asks her to present the launch plan, unchanged, at the board meeting on Thursday.

Ferraro sees the force of his argument. A delay has real costs for the company, for colleagues and possibly for patients who would benefit from faster triage. Yet she also knows that physicians under time pressure tend to trust the ranking, and that 'documenting it' would place the residual risk on the doctors who use the tool. Two of her team members have told her privately that they are uneasy; nobody has raised it with Bellandi.

Ferraro has 48 hours. She can present the plan as instructed, ask for a meeting with Bellandi before Thursday, propose a conditional launch, or take the finding to the company's quality and regulatory director. Each option has a cost, and each would change how she is seen as a leader.
