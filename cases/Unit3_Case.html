Harbourline Analytics: The Quiet Room

A newly promoted team lead in Rotterdam discovers that her team has stopped telling her things

Teaching case
The style you believe you lead with matters less than the style your people experience when you are under pressure.Core message · Unit 3 · ILO 1, 2

Lotte Vermeer was promoted fourteen months ago to lead the twelve-person pricing analytics team of a mid-sized freight-forwarding company in Rotterdam. She had been the best analyst on the team: fast, precise and impatient with sloppy work. On paper her first year was a success. Every quarterly forecast arrived on time, and the board praised the new pricing dashboard her team had built.

Then a large customer contract was priced on a fuel-cost assumption that two analysts had doubted for weeks. Neither of them raised it. When the error surfaced, it cost the company roughly €380,000 in margin over one quarter. In the review meeting one analyst said quietly: 'We assumed you had already checked it. And the last time someone questioned your numbers in a meeting, it did not go well.'

Lotte was shocked. She saw herself as open and fair. She had an open-door policy and closed every meeting with 'Any questions?' Her HR business partner showed her the latest engagement pulse. The team's score on 'I can raise concerns without negative consequences' had fallen from 4.1 to 2.9 out of 5 within a year, while 'My manager sets high standards' remained at 4.6.

Lotte describes her own style as coaching. Three team members, interviewed anonymously by HR, described something different: weekly reviews in which she redid their analyses overnight, corrections sent after midnight, and the question 'Who signed this off?' asked in front of the whole team. One said: 'She is brilliant, and she really cares about quality. But you learn not to bring her half-finished thinking.' Another added that the team now 'polishes everything before she sees it', which is why problems reach her late. Two of the strongest analysts have recently asked HR about internal transfers.

The operations director has offered Lotte the budget for an AI meeting assistant that transcribes team meetings, measures speaking time per person and flags 'negative sentiment' in voices. He is convinced it will show her who is disengaged. Lotte has two weeks before the next quarterly forecast. She must decide what to change in herself, in the way she runs meetings, and what to do about the tool.

Your tasks

  1. Which of Goleman's six styles does Lotte actually use under pressure, and which does she believe she uses? Cite evidence from the case.
  2. Which of the five components of emotional intelligence is her main gap, and how does it show in her behaviour?
  3. Which implicit voice theories are operating in the team? Name at least two and the leader behaviours that created them.
  4. Should Lotte deploy the AI meeting assistant with sentiment flags? Consider the EU AI Act and the likely effect on speaking up.
  5. Decide: write Lotte's plan for the next two weeks — three concrete behaviours from Edmondson's toolkit, and one thing she will stop doing.
