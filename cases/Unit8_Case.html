Velatra Insurance: The Claims Algorithm Says No

A claims team leader in Milan must lead people through automation — and decide when to overrule the machine

Teaching case
When machines take over tasks, people need more leadership, not less: meaning, fairness and judgement cannot be automated.Core message · Unit 8 · ILO 4, 1

Velatra Assicurazioni, a mid-sized insurer headquartered in Milan, introduced an AI claims-triage system in its motor and home-insurance division nine months ago. The system reads incoming claims, checks documents and photographs, scores the fraud risk and recommends whether to pay, investigate or reject. Since the launch, average handling time has fallen from 11 days to 4, and the board has asked for the same system to be rolled out to health claims next year.

Giulia Ferri leads a team of 14 claims handlers in the Milan office. Before the system, her handlers were experienced assessors who knew garages, surveyors and repeat claimants by name. Now around 70% of claims are processed automatically, and the handlers mainly review cases the system flags. Two of her most experienced people have asked to move to other departments; one told her, ‘I used to be the expert. Now I check the computer’s homework.’ Sick days in the team have risen by a third, and the latest engagement survey shows the lowest scores in the division for ‘my work is meaningful’.

Head office sees the numbers differently. The Chief Operating Officer wants Giulia to reduce the team to nine handlers by the end of the year and to stop the practice, started informally by her team, of re-checking every automatic rejection above €5,000. ‘The model is right 96% of the time,’ he says. ‘Your people are slowing it down.’

On Thursday afternoon a case lands on Giulia’s desk. The system has rejected a €38,000 home-damage claim from an 81-year-old customer in Bergamo as ‘high fraud risk’, mainly because the photographs were uploaded from a different address. The handler who reviewed it, Marco, believes the photographs were sent by the customer’s daughter, who lives in Brescia, and wants to overrule the rejection. The system’s documentation does not explain which factors drove the score. Under the internal policy, overruling the system requires a written justification, and every overruling is counted in the team’s monthly efficiency report.

Giulia must recommend to the COO by Monday how the team should work with the system from now on — including the headcount question, the re-checking practice and how decisions like Marco’s are to be made and owned.

Your tasks

  1. Diagnose the team’s situation with Self-Determination Theory and Bridges’ Transition Model. What exactly have the handlers lost?
  2. Does Velatra have meaningful human control over the claims system? Test tracking and tracing, and relate your answer to Article 14 of the EU AI Act.
  3. Should Marco’s overruling stand, and who should own the decision? Use one framework from Unit 5 (e.g. Kidder) to justify the ethical call.
  4. Choose a centaur or cyborg pattern for the team and redesign the handlers’ role with job crafting.
  5. Decide: what does Giulia recommend to the COO on headcount, re-checking and decision ownership? Write the recommendation in 200 words and state its core message in one sentence.
