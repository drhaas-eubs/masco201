Clara Ruiz at Valtera Foods: The Promotion Test

A newly promoted plant manager in Valencia discovers how her team really sees her

Teaching case
You cannot lead from a story you have never told yourself honestly — and others already see the version you avoid.Core message · Unit 2 · ILO 2

Valtera Foods is a mid-sized Spanish producer of chilled ready meals with plants in Valencia and Zaragoza and around 900 employees. Clara Ruiz, 34, joined the company as a quality engineer seven years ago and was promoted to plant manager in Valencia twelve months ago, the youngest person ever to hold the role. She describes herself as 'direct, fair and driven by quality'.

Her first year looked successful on paper. Customer complaints fell by 31%, an audit by the company's largest retail customer was passed without a major finding, and the plant hit its cost targets. Clara attributes this to clear standards and her willingness to stop a production line when quality was at risk, which she did eleven times during the year.

As part of the company's new leadership programme, Clara completed a 360-degree feedback survey. Her self-ratings were high on authenticity and on achieving results. The ratings from her eight direct reports told a different story. They rated her highly on achieving but low on relating and on listening, and several comments described a 'controlling' style: 'Decisions are made before the meeting starts', 'She is right about quality but never asks why we did what we did', and 'I would not tell her about a problem until I had already fixed it'.

Clara's first reaction was to dismiss the results as resistance to higher standards. She pasted the anonymised comments into an AI assistant and asked whether they reflected 'a normal reaction to a demanding manager'. The assistant reassured her that high-performing leaders often receive such feedback. A colleague from HR later pointed out that she had framed the question so that the answer could only confirm her view.

Two weeks later a line supervisor, Javier, resigned. In his exit interview he said he had raised a hygiene concern on the night shift three months earlier, been told to 'follow the procedure', and had stopped raising issues afterwards. The concern turned out to be justified; it was found during an internal audit, not through the team.

Clara now has to present her personal development plan to the operations director in four weeks. She is also aware that the operations director values exactly the quality focus that made her successful. She is unsure whether to change how she leads, to explain the feedback away, or to look more closely at which of her values are driving the behaviour her team experiences.

Your tasks

  1. Map Clara's self-description and her team's feedback onto the Leadership Circle Profile. Where is the gap?
  2. Which of Schwartz's values seem to drive Clara's behaviour under pressure, and which opposing value is neglected?
  3. Evaluate how Clara used the AI assistant. What would a sycophancy check have looked like?
  4. Could Javier's resignation become a crucible for Clara in Bennis and Thomas's sense? What meaning would she need to make of it?
  5. Write the two development priorities Clara should present to the operations director, with one verifiable checkpoint each.
