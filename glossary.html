Unit 1 · Leading in the Contemporary Business Environment

LeadershipUnit 1

The process of influencing others to understand and agree about what needs to be done and how to do it, and of facilitating individual and collective efforts to accomplish shared objectives.

Yukl, G.A. and Gardner, W.L. (2020) Leadership in Organizations. 9th edn, Global edn. Harlow: Pearson.
Trait approachUnit 1

A research tradition that explains leader effectiveness through relatively stable personal attributes such as energy, self-confidence and cognitive ability.

Yukl, G.A. and Gardner, W.L. (2020) Leadership in Organizations. 9th edn, Global edn. Harlow: Pearson.
Contingency theoryUnit 1

A family of leadership theories holding that the effectiveness of a leader's behaviour depends on features of the situation, such as the task, the followers and the leader's position power.

Yukl, G.A. and Gardner, W.L. (2020) Leadership in Organizations. 9th edn, Global edn. Harlow: Pearson.
Transformational leadershipUnit 1

Leadership that raises followers' motivation and performance beyond expectations by appealing to ideals and values, through idealised influence, inspiration, intellectual stimulation and individual consideration.

Bass, B.M. and Riggio, R.E. (2006) Transformational Leadership. 2nd edn. Mahwah, NJ: Lawrence Erlbaum Associates.
Adaptive problemUnit 1

A challenge for which no known solution exists and progress requires the people involved to change their values, beliefs or habits; contrasted with technical problems that experts can solve.

Heifetz, R.A. (1994) Leadership Without Easy Answers. Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press.
AI exposureUnit 1

The degree to which the tasks of an occupation could be performed substantially faster at equal quality with the help of AI systems such as large language models.

Eloundou, T., Manning, S., Mishkin, P. and Rock, D. (2024) 'GPTs are GPTs: labor market impact potential of LLMs', Science, 384(6702), pp. 1306-1308.

Unit 2 · The Leader’s Inner Game: Values, Identity and Authentic Leadership

Authentic leadershipUnit 2

A pattern of leader behaviour grounded in self-awareness, a clear set of internalised values and transparency in relationships, through which leaders lead from their own life story rather than imitating others.

George, B., Sims, P., McLean, A.N. and Mayer, D. (2007) 'Discovering your authentic leadership', Harvard Business Review, 85(2), pp. 129-138.
True NorthUnit 2

The internal compass of beliefs, values and principles, derived from a leader's life story, that guides their decisions and behaviour, especially under pressure.

George, B. (2015) Discover Your True North. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley.
CrucibleUnit 2

An intense, often traumatic experience that tests a person and, when meaning is made from it, transforms their sense of identity and their capacity to lead.

Bennis, W.G. and Thomas, R.J. (2002) 'Crucibles of leadership', Harvard Business Review, 80(9), pp. 39-45.
Adaptive capacityUnit 2

The ability to understand context quickly, recognise and seize opportunities and persist through setbacks; identified by Bennis and Thomas as the essential skill that crucibles develop.

Bennis, W.G. and Thomas, R.J. (2002) 'Crucibles of leadership', Harvard Business Review, 80(9), pp. 39-45.
Basic valuesUnit 2

Trans-situational goals of varying importance that serve as guiding principles in a person's life; Schwartz identifies ten such values organised in a circular structure of compatibility and conflict.

Schwartz, S.H. (2012) 'An overview of the Schwartz theory of basic values', Online Readings in Psychology and Culture, 2(1), article 11.
360-degree feedbackUnit 2

A structured process in which a person receives anonymous ratings and comments on their behaviour from multiple sources, such as manager, peers and direct reports, compared with their self-assessment.

Anderson, R.J. and Adams, W.A. (2016) Mastering Leadership: An Integrated Framework for Breakthrough Performance and Extraordinary Business Results. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley.

Unit 3 · Leadership Self-Diagnosis: Emotional Intelligence & Psychological Safety

Emotional intelligenceUnit 3

The ability to monitor one's own and others' feelings and emotions, to discriminate among them and to use this information to guide one's thinking and actions.

Salovey, P. and Mayer, J.D. (1990) 'Emotional intelligence', Imagination, Cognition and Personality, 9(3), pp. 185-211.
Leadership styleUnit 3

A characteristic pattern of leader behaviour, drawing on specific emotional intelligence competencies, that shapes how a leader directs, motivates and relates to people.

Goleman, D. (2000) 'Leadership that gets results', Harvard Business Review, 78(2), pp. 78-90.
Organisational climateUnit 3

The shared perception of the working environment — flexibility, responsibility, standards, rewards, clarity and commitment — through which a leader's style affects performance.

Goleman, D. (2000) 'Leadership that gets results', Harvard Business Review, 78(2), pp. 78-90.
Psychological safetyUnit 3

A shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking, such as asking questions, admitting mistakes or voicing disagreement.

Edmondson, A.C. (1999) 'Psychological safety and learning behavior in work teams', Administrative Science Quarterly, 44(2), pp. 350-383.
Implicit voice theoriesUnit 3

Taken-for-granted beliefs about when and why speaking up to those in authority is risky, which lead employees to censor themselves even without explicit threats.

Detert, J.R. and Edmondson, A.C. (2011) 'Implicit voice theories: taken-for-granted rules of self-censorship at work', Academy of Management Journal, 54(3), pp. 461-488.
Affective computingUnit 3

Computing that relates to, arises from or deliberately influences emotion, including systems that attempt to recognise or simulate human affect.

Picard, R.W. (1997) Affective Computing. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.

Unit 4 · Navigating Challenging Dilemmas: From Technical Fixes to Adaptive Leadership

Adaptive challengeUnit 4

A problem that cannot be solved by existing expertise or authority because it requires the people involved to learn and to change their values, beliefs, habits or loyalties.

Heifetz, R.A. (1994) Leadership Without Easy Answers. Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press.
Technical problemUnit 4

A problem that is clearly defined and can be solved by applying existing knowledge, procedures and authority.

Heifetz, R.A. (1994) Leadership Without Easy Answers. Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press.
Cynefin domainUnit 4

One of the decision contexts — simple, complicated, complex, chaotic or disorder — distinguished by the relationship between cause and effect, each calling for a different leadership response.

Snowden, D.J. and Boone, M.E. (2007) 'A leader's framework for decision making', Harvard Business Review, 85(11), pp. 68-76.
Crisis responsibilityUnit 4

The degree to which stakeholders believe an organisation caused a crisis, which determines the reputational threat and the appropriate response strategy.

Coombs, W.T. (2007) 'Protecting organization reputations during a crisis: the development and application of situational crisis communication theory', Corporate Reputation Review, 10(3), pp. 163-176.
Level of automationUnit 4

The degree to which a machine rather than a human performs a given stage of information processing or action, ranging from fully manual to fully automatic.

Parasuraman, R., Sheridan, T.B. and Wickens, C.D. (2000) 'A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation', IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics - Part A: Systems and Humans, 30(3), pp. 286-297.
Automation complacencyUnit 4

Insufficient monitoring and checking of automated systems by human operators, especially when the automation is usually reliable and attention is in demand elsewhere.

Parasuraman, R. and Manzey, D.H. (2010) 'Complacency and bias in human use of automation: an attentional integration', Human Factors, 52(3), pp. 381-410.

Unit 5 · Moral Courage & Ethical Leadership Under Pressure

Moral sensitivityUnit 5

The ability to recognise that a situation contains an ethical issue and to see how possible actions would affect the interests of others.

Rest, J.R. (1986) Moral Development: Advances in Research and Theory. New York: Praeger.
Right-versus-right dilemmaUnit 5

An ethical dilemma in which two genuinely held core values conflict, so that both available courses of action can be morally defended.

Kidder, R.M. (1995) How Good People Make Tough Choices: Resolving the Dilemmas of Ethical Living. New York: William Morrow.
Moral courageUnit 5

The readiness to endure danger, such as loss of status, income or relationships, for the sake of moral principle.

Kidder, R.M. (2005) Moral Courage. New York: William Morrow.
Upward voiceUnit 5

The discretionary communication of ideas, concerns or disagreement by employees to people with more formal power in the organisation, with the intent of improving the situation.

Detert, J.R. and Burris, E.R. (2007) 'Leadership behavior and employee voice: is the door really open?', Academy of Management Journal, 50(4), pp. 869-884.
Responsibility gapUnit 5

The situation in which no human agent can fairly be held fully responsible for the actions of a learning system, because no one could adequately predict or control them.

Matthias, A. (2004) 'The responsibility gap: ascribing responsibility for the actions of learning automata', Ethics and Information Technology, 6(3), pp. 175-183.
High-risk AI systemUnit 5

Under the EU AI Act, an AI system that poses significant risks to health, safety or fundamental rights and is therefore subject to strict obligations such as risk management, data governance and human oversight.

European Parliament and Council of the European Union (2024) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act). Official Journal of the European Union, L, 12 July 2024.

Unit 6 · Leading With and Through Stakeholders in Hybrid and Remote Environments

StakeholderUnit 6

Any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of an organisation's objectives.

Freeman, R.E. (1984) Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach. Boston, MA: Pitman.
Stakeholder salienceUnit 6

The degree to which managers give priority to competing stakeholder claims, determined by the stakeholder's power, legitimacy and urgency.

Mitchell, R.K., Agle, B.R. and Wood, D.J. (1997) 'Toward a theory of stakeholder identification and salience: defining the principle of who and what really counts', Academy of Management Review, 22(4), pp. 853-886.
Definitive stakeholderUnit 6

A stakeholder holding power, legitimacy and urgency at the same time, whose claims managers must address immediately.

Mitchell, R.K., Agle, B.R. and Wood, D.J. (1997) 'Toward a theory of stakeholder identification and salience: defining the principle of who and what really counts', Academy of Management Review, 22(4), pp. 853-886.
Media richnessUnit 6

The capacity of a communication medium to convey information through immediate feedback, multiple cues, natural language and personal focus.

Daft, R.L. and Lengel, R.H. (1986) 'Organizational information requirements, media richness and structural design', Management Science, 32(5), pp. 554-571.
Virtual distanceUnit 6

The perceived psychological and emotional distance that develops when people interact mainly through technology, made up of physical, operational and affinity distance.

Sobel Lojeski, K. and Reilly, R.R. (2008) Uniting the Virtual Workforce: Transforming Leadership and Innovation in the Globally Integrated Enterprise. Hoboken, NJ: John Wiley & Sons.
ExplainabilityUnit 6

The extent to which the reasons behind an AI system's output can be made understandable to the people who use it or are affected by it.

Gunning, D., Stefik, M., Choi, J., Miller, T., Stumpf, S. and Yang, G.-Z. (2019) 'XAI—Explainable artificial intelligence', Science Robotics, 4(37), eaay7120.

Unit 7 · Earned Authority: Leadership Presence and Credibility Without a Title

Personal powerUnit 7

Influence that rests on others’ perception of a person’s expertise, information or character (expert, informational and referent power) and travels with the person rather than the role.

Yukl, G.A. and Gardner, W.L. (2020) Leadership in Organizations. 9th edn, Global edn. Harlow: Pearson.
Referent powerUnit 7

Influence based on others’ identification with, liking of or respect for a person, built through consistent and trustworthy behaviour over time.

French, J.R.P. and Raven, B. (1959) 'The bases of social power', in Cartwright, D. (ed.) Studies in Social Power. Ann Arbor, MI: Institute for Social Research, pp. 150-167.
EthosUnit 7

In Aristotle’s rhetoric, the persuasive force of the speaker’s perceived character — practical wisdom, virtue and goodwill towards the audience.

Aristotle (1991) On Rhetoric: A Theory of Civic Discourse. Translated by G.A. Kennedy. New York: Oxford University Press.
CredibilityUnit 7

The degree to which others believe a person is honest, well-intentioned and competent, and therefore worth trusting and following.

Kouzes, J.M. and Posner, B.Z. (2017) The Leadership Challenge. 6th edn. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley.
Executive presenceUnit 7

The combination of gravitas, communication and appearance through which others judge whether a person is leadership material.

Hewlett, S.A. (2014) Executive Presence: The Missing Link Between Merit and Success. New York: Harper Business.
Social presenceUnit 7

The degree to which a communication medium conveys the salience of the other person and the interpersonal relationship between the communicators.

Short, J., Williams, E. and Christie, B. (1976) The Social Psychology of Telecommunications. London: Wiley.

Unit 8 · Leading in the Age of AI: Human Leadership When Machines Are in the Room

Basic psychological needsUnit 8

In Self-Determination Theory, the universal needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness whose satisfaction supports intrinsic motivation and well-being.

Ryan, R.M. and Deci, E.L. (2000) 'Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being', American Psychologist, 55(1), pp. 68-78.
Neutral zoneUnit 8

The middle phase of a transition, after the old way has ended and before the new one is established, marked by uncertainty but also by openness to innovation.

Bridges, W. (1991) Managing Transitions: Making the Most of Change. Reading, MA: Addison-Wesley.
Job craftingUnit 8

Self-initiated changes that employees make to the tasks, relationships and meaning of their work.

Wrzesniewski, A. and Dutton, J.E. (2001) 'Crafting a job: revisioning employees as active crafters of their work', Academy of Management Review, 26(2), pp. 179-201.
Jagged technological frontierUnit 8

The uneven boundary of AI capability, under which tasks of similar apparent difficulty fall either inside (AI helps) or outside (AI harms quality) what current models can do well.

Dell'Acqua, F., McFowland III, E., Mollick, E.R., Lifshitz-Assaf, H., Kellogg, K., Rajendran, S., Krayer, L., Candelon, F. and Lakhani, K.R. (2023) Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier: Field Experimental Evidence of the Effects of AI on Knowledge Worker Productivity and Quality. Harvard Business School Working Paper No. 24-013.
Meaningful human controlUnit 8

Control over an autonomous system that satisfies both tracking (it responds to relevant human moral reasons) and tracing (outcomes can be attributed to an accountable person).

Santoni de Sio, F. and van den Hoven, J. (2018) 'Meaningful human control over autonomous systems: a philosophical account', Frontiers in Robotics and AI, 5, article 15.
Human oversightUnit 8

The EU AI Act requirement that high-risk AI systems allow natural persons to understand, monitor, interpret and, where necessary, override or stop them.

European Parliament and Council of the European Union (2024) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act), Article 14. Official Journal of the European Union, L, 12 July 2024.
