The problem is rarely not knowing

Leadership education has long treated ethics as a reasoning problem. Give managers better theories — utilitarian, deontological, virtue-based — and they will reach better conclusions. The difficulty is that most ethical failures in organisations do not begin with a reasoning error. They begin with people who sense that something is wrong and say nothing, or say it so softly that nobody has to hear it.

Research on organisational silence supports this view. Morrison and Milliken (2000) describe silence as a collective phenomenon: when employees believe that speaking up is futile or dangerous, and when managers fear negative feedback, organisations systematically withhold information about problems. Detert and Burris (2007) found that employees' willingness to raise concerns depends strongly on how they perceive their manager's openness and the personal risk of speaking. Silence, in other words, is a rational response to a system, not simply a personal weakness.

The Volkswagen emissions case is a well-documented example. In 2015 the company admitted that software installed in diesel vehicles detected test conditions and altered emissions performance. The engineering knowledge to recognise the problem existed inside the company for years. What was missing was not information but voice that reached, and was heard by, the people able to stop it.

From compliance to courage

Compliance asks: is this allowed? Ethics asks: is this right? Moral courage asks a third, harder question: am I willing to act on what is right when it costs me something? Rest's (1986) four-component model makes the distinction precise. Ethical action requires moral sensitivity (noticing that ethics is at stake), moral judgement (deciding what is right), moral motivation (giving ethical values priority over competing ones such as career or loyalty) and moral character (the persistence to follow through). Most training targets the first two components. Most failures happen at the last two.

Kidder (1995) adds a second insight. The hardest dilemmas are not right versus wrong but right versus right: truth against loyalty, the individual against the community, the short term against the long term, justice against mercy. A product lead who owes loyalty to the manager who promoted her, and truth to the patients who rely on her product, faces two legitimate claims. Recognising this protects leaders from two errors: treating every disagreement as a moral scandal, and dismissing real dilemmas as mere business trade-offs.

Kidder (2005) later defined moral courage as the meeting point of three elements: commitment to principles, awareness of the danger involved, and the willingness to endure it. Principles without danger are easy virtue. Danger without principles is bravado. Only when all three are present does courage appear — and because each element can be strengthened deliberately, courage becomes something that can be developed rather than something people simply have or lack.

Challenging upward as a skill

Gentile's (2010) Giving Voice to Values changes the question from 'What is the right thing to do?' to 'Once I know what is right, how do I get it done effectively?' The approach starts from a realistic assumption: most professionals will face situations where their values conflict with what they are asked to do, and such conflict is normal rather than exceptional. Preparation therefore matters more than heroism.

In practice this means scripting and rehearsal. A leader who wants to challenge a superior anticipates the reasons and rationalisations she will hear — 'everyone does it', 'it is not your responsibility', 'we will fix it later' — and prepares responses grounded in evidence and in the other person's own goals. She chooses the right moment, the right channel and a proposal rather than only an objection. Rehearsed aloud, with peers playing the sceptic, the script becomes available under pressure, when improvisation tends to fail.

Three practical rules follow. First, frame the concern in terms of shared purpose: protecting the launch, the customer or the company's licence to operate. Second, bring a constructive option, such as a conditional launch, an additional control or a short delay with a clear plan. Third, escalate in steps: speak to the person first, then use formal channels if the concern is not addressed. Challenging upward is not disloyalty; done well, it is one of the most valuable forms of loyalty a leader can offer.

When the algorithm is part of the dilemma

AI adds a new layer. When a learning system contributes to a harmful outcome, it is often unclear who is responsible: the developer, the deploying company or the operator who accepted the recommendation. Matthias (2004) called this the responsibility gap. Elish (2019) showed that in practice blame often falls on the human closest to the system — the 'moral crumple zone' — even when that person had little real control. For leaders, the lesson is that responsibility must be assigned deliberately before deployment, not argued about after an incident. Speaking up about AI risk is therefore not a technical side issue; it is part of the leader's ethical role.

The EU AI Act makes this concrete. Systems classified as high risk — including many used in health, employment and critical infrastructure — must be designed for effective human oversight, with documented risk management and data governance. The High-Level Expert Group's seven requirements for trustworthy AI go further and ask for transparency, fairness and accountability across the whole life cycle. For a leader, these are not only compliance checklists. They give her a shared, legitimate language for raising a concern upward: 'this is a high-risk system, and we have a gap in data governance' is harder to dismiss than 'I have a bad feeling'. Preparing that language in advance is exactly what Giving Voice to Values asks of her.

Moral courage is not a personality trait but a rehearsed skill: diagnose the dilemma, script your voice, and bring a proposal when you challenge upward.
