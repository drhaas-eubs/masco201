A century of partial answers

Leadership is one of the most studied subjects in the social sciences, and one of the least settled. Every generation of researchers has asked a slightly different question and, in answering it, has left something important out. The early trait researchers asked who leaders are. They searched for the qualities — intelligence, dominance, self-confidence, energy — that separate leaders from followers. They found some consistent associations, but no trait guaranteed effectiveness, and the same person could succeed in one setting and fail in another.

The behavioural researchers of the 1950s and 1960s changed the question to what effective leaders do. Studies at Ohio State and Michigan identified two broad families of behaviour: one concerned with the task, the other with people. This was a real advance, because behaviour can be observed, trained and changed in a way that personality cannot. But it still assumed that one profile of behaviour would work everywhere.

Contingency theories corrected that assumption by asking when particular behaviours work. Fiedler, Hersey and Blanchard, and House's path–goal theory all argued that the right behaviour depends on the followers, the task and the leader's position. Transformational theories, from Burns and later Bass, then asked how leaders change what followers value, raising their motivation beyond the exchange of reward for effort. Most recently, adaptive and shared approaches ask how leadership works when problems have no known solution and influence is distributed across a network rather than held by one person.

Reading theories as hypotheses

Yukl's synthesis of this literature makes a point that is easy to overlook: each school captured something real, and none is complete. The useful question is therefore not which theory is right, but what each one claims and under which conditions the claim holds. Treated this way, a theory is a hypothesis about people, not a recipe.

This is where the first-principles method from MASCO101 becomes a leadership tool. Reduce a theory to its foundational premises and examine each one. Trait theory assumes that stable personal qualities predict effectiveness across settings. Contingency theory assumes that situations can be classified reliably enough to choose a style in advance. Transformational theory assumes that followers want to be inspired and that inspiration translates into performance. Each premise can be tested against evidence, and each fails in some conditions. A leader who knows where a theory's premises break is far better equipped than one who has memorised its labels.

Yukl's hierarchical taxonomy offers a practical bridge between the schools. It groups effective leader behaviour into four meta-categories: task-oriented behaviour such as planning and monitoring; relations-oriented behaviour such as supporting and developing; change-oriented behaviour such as envisioning and encouraging innovation; and external behaviour such as networking and representing the unit. The taxonomy does not tell a leader what to do; it gives them a vocabulary for diagnosing what a situation needs and noticing which behaviours they habitually neglect.

What changes when the machine arrives

The contemporary business environment adds a pressure no earlier theory anticipated. Many of the task-oriented behaviours in Yukl's first category — scheduling, monitoring, reporting, even drafting plans — are precisely the tasks that AI systems now perform well. Research on task-based AI exposure suggests that knowledge work is affected task by task rather than job by job, and field experiments show that AI helps strongly on some tasks while quietly degrading performance on others that look similar.

One reading of this is that leadership becomes less necessary because the system does the managing. The more convincing reading is the opposite. When routine coordination is automated, what remains for the person in charge is concentrated in the other three categories: building trust and commitment, leading change that people resist, and representing the unit to stakeholders who expect a human face. Kotter's distinction between management, which copes with complexity, and leadership, which copes with change, becomes sharper rather than obsolete.

Heifetz adds a further distinction that the course will develop in Unit 4. Technical problems have known solutions that an expert or a system can apply. Adaptive problems require people to change their values, habits or loyalties, and no one can solve them on others' behalf. AI is a powerful engine for technical work. It cannot do the adaptive work for a team, and leaders who treat adaptive challenges as technical ones — by installing a dashboard where a conversation was needed — are among the most common causes of failure.

Using theory well

None of this makes classical theory redundant. It makes it more valuable, provided it is used critically. Students in this course will meet many frameworks. The discipline the course asks for is the same each time: identify the premise, test it against evidence and the situation, and decide deliberately whether to keep, adapt or set it aside. That discipline, not any single theory, is the beginning of a leadership edge.

Before Unit 2, notice one leader you work with or have worked with, and ask which of Yukl's four families of behaviour they rely on most and which they avoid. That observation is the raw material for the self-diagnosis the course turns to next, because the same question applies to you.

Treat every leadership theory as a hypothesis to be tested against the situation, not as an answer to be applied.
