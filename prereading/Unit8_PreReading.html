When machines take over tasks, people need more leadership, not less: meaning, fairness and judgement cannot be automated.Core message · Unit 8 · ILO 4, 1
Key framework
Self-Determination Theory · Meaningful Human Control

The tasks go; the people stay

Every wave of automation has been sold with the same promise: machines take the routine work, and people move on to more interesting things. Generative AI makes the promise more plausible than ever. In a large field study of customer-support agents, Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2023) found that access to an AI assistant raised productivity by around 14% on average, with the biggest gains for the least experienced workers. In a field experiment with consultants, Dell’Acqua and colleagues (2023) found large quality gains on tasks inside what they called the ‘jagged technological frontier’ — and lower accuracy on a task just outside it.

What these studies measure is tasks. What leaders manage is people, and people do not experience automation as a redistribution of tasks. They experience it as a change to what they are good at, whom they work with and what their work means. A claims assessor whose expertise is now embedded in a model, or an analyst whose first draft is now written by a tool, has not merely gained time. She may have lost the part of the job that made her feel competent and recognised.

Self-Determination Theory gives this loss a precise form. Ryan and Deci (2000) argue that people are intrinsically motivated when three needs are met: autonomy (a sense of choice in what they do), competence (a sense of mastery) and relatedness (connection to others). Automation can strengthen all three — or starve them. A rollout that is imposed, measures people against the machine’s speed and removes the conversations through which expertise was shared hits all three needs at once. The predictable result is not resistance to technology but loss of motivation, which looks like resistance from the outside.

Leading the transition, not only the change

William Bridges drew a distinction that matters here. Change is situational: the new system goes live on a given date. Transition is psychological: the slow process by which people let go of an old identity, pass through a confusing ‘neutral zone’ and commit to a new beginning. Organisations plan the change and neglect the transition, which is why so many technically successful projects fail on the people side. Kotter’s eight steps make the same point from the organisational level: urgency and vision are not enough if short-term wins and cultural anchoring are skipped.

For a leader, the practical consequence is simple and uncomfortable: name the ending before you sell the beginning. People need to hear what they are losing from someone who takes it seriously. Then they need a real say in how the new work is designed. Job crafting research (Wrzesniewski and Dutton, 2001) shows that employees actively reshape their tasks, relationships and the meaning they attach to their work. A leader who invites a team to redesign its own role around AI — which tasks to hand over, which to keep, what new expertise to build — turns automation from something done to people into something done by them.

Who answers when the algorithm decides?

The second leadership problem is accountability. Many organisations claim to keep a ‘human in the loop’. The phrase is reassuring and often empty. Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018) argue that meaningful human control requires two conditions. Tracking: the system must respond to the moral reasons of the people who design and use it. Tracing: every outcome must be traceable to at least one person who understands the system and accepts responsibility for it. A reviewer who sees only a score, cannot see why it was produced and is penalised for disagreeing satisfies neither condition. She is in the loop, but she is not in control.

European law now makes part of this explicit. Article 14 of the EU Artificial Intelligence Act requires that high-risk AI systems — including many used in employment and access to essential services — be designed so that humans can understand their limits, stay aware of automation bias, interpret the output and override or stop the system. Law can require the stop button. Only leadership can create a culture in which people dare to press it.

This is where the course comes together. Deciding when to overrule the algorithm is an adaptive challenge, not a technical one (Unit 4). It is often a right-versus-right dilemma between efficiency and fairness to an individual (Unit 5). It needs moral courage to challenge upward, the credibility to be heard without a title (Unit 7) and the emotional intelligence to keep a team engaged while its work is changing (Unit 3). De Cremer (2024) puts the point bluntly: AI adoption is a leadership question before it is a technology question. Mollick (2024) adds that the people closest to the work are usually the first to discover what AI is good and bad at. The leader’s task is to keep them motivated enough to say so — and accountable enough to act on it.

Automation removes tasks, not the need for leadership: someone must still give people meaning and answer for the machine’s decisions.
