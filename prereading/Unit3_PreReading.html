The self-perception gap

Ask a room of managers to describe their leadership style and most will choose words such as coaching, democratic or supportive. Ask the people who work for them and the picture changes. Leaders judge themselves by their intentions; everyone else judges them by their behaviour, and above all by their behaviour on a bad day. This gap is not a moral failing. It is a predictable consequence of position: the more power you hold, the less honest feedback reaches you, and the more your own account of yourself goes unchallenged.

Daniel Goleman's work on emotional intelligence offers a way into this gap. In his 1998 Harvard Business Review article he argued that what separates outstanding leaders from competent ones is less IQ or technical skill than five capabilities: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skill. The first of these carries the others. A leader who cannot accurately read their own moods, and the effect those moods have on other people, cannot regulate them, cannot see how others are reacting and cannot adjust. Emotional intelligence, in this view, is not a soft extra. It is the instrument through which every other leadership skill is delivered.

Six styles, one default

Two years later Goleman translated the idea into behaviour. Drawing on a large consultancy dataset of executives, 'Leadership that gets results' (2000) described six styles. The coercive leader demands immediate compliance. The authoritative leader mobilises people towards a vision. The affiliative leader builds harmony and emotional bonds. The democratic leader forges consensus through participation. The pacesetting leader sets high standards and models them personally. The coaching leader develops people for the future. Each style draws on different emotional intelligence competencies, and each has a distinct effect on the climate of a team.

Three findings matter most for this unit. First, the authoritative style had the most positive effect on climate, while coercive and pacesetting styles, used as defaults, had negative effects. Second, the leaders with the best climate and results were not those with one perfect style but those who had mastered four or more and switched between them as the situation demanded. Third, the styles that damage climate are precisely those that feel most natural under pressure. When a deadline looms, the expert who has been promoted tends to take the work back, set the pace and tell people what to do. It feels like responsibility. To the team it feels like a verdict on their competence.

This is why the Six Styles self-assessment in your dossier asks for three answers, not one: your dominant style, your back-up style under pressure, and the style you rarely or never use. The missing style is often the most useful information you will collect this term.

Silence is something leaders teach

Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety began with a puzzle. In a study of hospital teams, the better-led units appeared to report more medication errors than the weaker ones. The explanation was not that they made more mistakes, but that people in those units felt safe enough to report them. Psychological safety, the shared belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks such as asking a question, admitting an error or disagreeing, turned out to be a condition for learning rather than a reward for comfort.

In MASCO301 you will study psychological safety as a property of teams. Here the focus is narrower and more personal: what you, as an individual leader, do that creates or destroys it. Edmondson's toolkit names three moves. Set the stage by framing work as uncertain and interdependent, so that speaking up is part of the job rather than a favour. Invite participation through situational humility and genuine questions, not the ritual 'Any questions?' at the end of a meeting. And respond productively when someone brings bad news or disagrees: thank them, then deal with the problem. The third move is the decisive one. People decide whether to speak up a second time by watching what happened the first time.

James Detert and Edmondson added an uncomfortable finding. Even under leaders who sincerely want openness, employees carry implicit voice theories, taken-for-granted rules such as 'do not embarrass the boss in public' or 'only speak up if you have solid data'. These rules are learned over whole careers and can be triggered by a single raised eyebrow. A leader cannot simply declare them void. They must be named, tested and disproved through repeated behaviour.

What AI can and cannot see

It is tempting to hand the self-diagnosis to technology. Meeting tools can now count speaking time, transcribe every word and attach sentiment labels to voices. Some of this can genuinely help a leader notice that one person always speaks first and three never speak at all. But two limits apply. Psychologically, research summarised by Barrett and colleagues (2019) shows that emotions cannot be reliably read from facial movements alone, so a 'negative sentiment' flag is an inference, not a fact. Legally, the EU AI Act has prohibited AI systems that infer the emotions of people in the workplace since February 2025, except for medical or safety reasons. A leader who monitors their team's emotions with AI is not building psychological safety; they are demonstrating why silence is the rational choice.

The more useful role for AI in this unit is as a sparring partner on case material: let it propose which style a leader is using, then argue with it from the evidence. The judgement about your own behaviour, and the courage to ask others for theirs, remains yours.

Your team does not experience your intentions; it experiences your default style under pressure and your reaction to bad news — so that is what you must diagnose.
