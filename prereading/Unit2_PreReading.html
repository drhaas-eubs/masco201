The problem with borrowed leadership

Most people's first theory of leadership is borrowed. They model themselves on a manager they admired, a public figure they read about, or a set of competencies listed in a job advertisement. Borrowed leadership can work for a while, especially when conditions are calm. It tends to fail when conditions become difficult, because a style copied from someone else gives no guidance on what to do when two good options collide or when doing the right thing carries a personal cost.

Bill George, a former chief executive of Medtronic who later taught at Harvard Business School, came to this conclusion through research rather than intuition. With colleagues he interviewed 125 leaders about how they had developed. The researchers expected to find a common set of traits or a shared style. They did not. What the leaders had in common was that they led from their own life story: they had made sense of the experiences that shaped them and used that understanding to decide what they stood for. George called this direction a leader's True North.

Five dimensions of a True North

George and his colleagues describe authentic leadership development through five dimensions. Self-awareness comes first: knowing one's story, strengths, weaknesses and the triggers that pull one off course. Values and principles follow, but George insists that values are only known once they have been tested; a value one has never paid for is a preference, not a principle. Motivations come next, and George distinguishes extrinsic motivations such as status and money from intrinsic ones such as personal growth and meaning, warning that leaders who chase only extrinsic rewards are easily derailed. The fourth dimension is a support team — people who will tell the leader the truth. The fifth is an integrated life, in which the person at work and the person at home are recognisably the same.

Values are the dimension most often misunderstood. Shalom Schwartz's cross-cultural research shows that almost everyone holds the same ten basic values to some degree — among them achievement, benevolence, security, self-direction and universalism. What differs between people is the priority they give them. Schwartz arranges the values in a circle so that neighbouring values support each other while opposing values compete: achievement and power stand against benevolence and universalism; openness to change stands against tradition and security. A leader's character becomes visible when circumstances force a choice between opposing values, which is why values are only truly visible under pressure.

Crucibles and the stories we tell

Warren Bennis and Robert Thomas studied leaders across two generations and found that almost all of them could point to an intense, often painful experience that had changed them: a failure, a loss, an early responsibility that came too soon. They called these experiences crucibles. Crucibles do not automatically create leaders. What matters is whether the person makes meaning from the experience and emerges with greater adaptive capacity — the ability to read new situations and adjust — rather than with bitterness or rigidity.

This has a practical consequence for students. Assessment 2 asks for a crucible analysis of a real experience. The point is not to produce a dramatic story but to show how an experience changed one's understanding of oneself and what one now does differently. A modest crucible analysed honestly is worth more than a dramatic one retold as a success story.

The gap others can see

Self-knowledge has a limit that reflection alone cannot remove. People judge themselves by their intentions; others judge them by their behaviour. Instruments such as the Leadership Circle Profile, developed by Robert Anderson and William Adams, make this gap visible by comparing self-ratings with ratings from colleagues, and by linking visible behaviour to underlying tendencies such as controlling, protecting or complying. A leader who sees herself as decisive may be experienced as controlling; one who sees himself as considerate may be experienced as conflict-avoidant.

AI tools can help with reflection by asking good questions, but they introduce two risks. The first is disclosure: reflection involves sensitive information about oneself and others, and data minimisation should be decided before any tool is used. The second is flattery: language models have been shown to agree with users' stated views. A reflection partner that always agrees cannot close the gap between intention and impact. Only feedback from people who experience one's leadership can do that, which is why this unit ends with the design of a 360-degree feedback exercise.

The work of this unit is therefore deliberately unfinished. The values map, the crucible draft and the True North compass you produce today are first versions. They become reliable only when they have been tested against the experience of others and revised. Treat the 360 feedback not as a verdict on your character but as data about the distance between the leader you intend to be and the leader people meet. Closing that distance, step by verifiable step, is what the 12-month development plan in Assessment 2 is for.

Authenticity is not being yourself; it is knowing which values you will pay for and checking how others experience the way you live them.
