The comfort of the technical fix

When something goes wrong, people look to the person in charge for answers. Ronald Heifetz argues that this expectation is precisely what leads leaders astray. Some problems are technical: the problem is clearly defined, the solution is known, and someone with the right expertise and authority can implement it. A broken production line, a missing compliance document and a failed server are technical problems. Other problems are adaptive: they cannot be solved by expertise alone because they require the people involved to change their values, beliefs, habits or loyalties. A culture in which employees hide mistakes, a business model eroded by new technology or a team divided by a merger are adaptive challenges.

Most real crises contain both. The danger, in Heifetz and Linsky's words, is that the most common cause of leadership failure is treating adaptive challenges as if they were technical problems. The reason is understandable. Technical fixes are fast, visible and within a leader's authority. They relieve the pressure on the leader and give everyone a sense that something is being done. Adaptive work is slow, uncertain and painful, because it asks people to accept loss. Replacing a sensor is easy; asking a production culture to value caution over output is not.

Adaptive leadership therefore changes the leader's job. Instead of providing the answer, the leader identifies the adaptive challenge, regulates the heat so that people stay engaged without being overwhelmed, keeps attention on the hard issues and gives the work back to the people who must change. To do this the leader must regularly step off the dance floor of action and onto the balcony, to see patterns such as avoidance, scapegoating or a rush to a familiar solution.

Context decides the decision mode

If Heifetz asks what kind of problem you face, Dave Snowden and Mary Boone ask what kind of context you are in. You met the Cynefin framework in MASCO101 as a thinking tool; in this unit you use it as a leader in a crisis. In simple contexts cause and effect are obvious, and the leader can sense, categorise and respond with best practice. In complicated contexts cause and effect can be discovered by analysis, so the leader senses, analyses and responds with expert help. In complex contexts cause and effect are visible only in hindsight; the leader must probe with safe-to-fail experiments, sense what emerges and amplify what works. In chaotic contexts there is no time to find patterns: the leader must act to establish order, then sense and respond.

Two lessons follow for crisis leadership. First, crises move between domains. The first hours of a product recall are often chaotic and require decisive command; by the following week the underlying problem is usually complex and requires experiments and participation. A leader who keeps commanding after the chaos has passed suppresses the learning the organisation needs. Second, the worst errors occur when leaders apply the methods of one domain to another, for instance treating a complex cultural problem as a complicated engineering one, which is exactly the technical fix Heifetz warns against.

Keeping trust when things go wrong

A crisis is also a test of credibility. Timothy Coombs' Situational Crisis Communication Theory proposes that the reputational threat of a crisis depends on how much responsibility stakeholders attribute to the organisation. Where the organisation is seen as a victim, providing information and expressing concern may be enough. Where the crisis is seen as an accident, explaining and diminishing responsibility may be acceptable. Where it is seen as preventable, only rebuild strategies — apology and compensation — protect trust. A history of similar crises or a poor prior reputation intensifies the threat. In every case, the first duty is to give stakeholders the information they need to protect themselves.

The theory explains why statements that minimise responsibility so often backfire. When stakeholders later learn that warning signs were ignored, a crisis they first read as an accident is reclassified as preventable, and the earlier minimising statement becomes a second crisis of honesty.

Machines in the crisis room

AI tools are increasingly present in crisis decisions, from quality monitoring to drafting statements. Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens remind us that automation is not all-or-nothing: information acquisition, analysis, decision selection and action can each be automated to a different degree. Human responses to such systems are themselves biased. People tend to abandon an algorithm after seeing it err, even when it outperforms humans, while non-experts may follow algorithmic advice too readily. A system that produces many false alarms teaches its users to ignore it, which is a human and organisational problem as much as a technical one.

In the Crisis Room you will therefore make three leadership decisions about technology: how much of each decision to automate, how to use AI to attack your own plan rather than to confirm it, and whether a tool fits the domain you are in. The underlying question stays the same throughout the unit: is this something a better tool can fix, or something people must change?

Before you act in a crisis, ask whether an expert can fix the problem or whether the people involved must change — then choose the decision mode the context demands.
