The authority you borrow and the authority you earn

Most people who complete a master’s degree will spend their first years leading without the title to match. They coordinate projects across departments, persuade colleagues senior to them and represent their team to clients who have never met them. In each case the organisation chart gives them almost nothing to work with. Whether they get things done depends on a different kind of authority: the kind that people grant because they choose to.

John French and Bertram Raven gave this observation its classic form in 1959. They distinguished five bases of social power — legitimate, reward, coercive, expert and referent — and Raven later added a sixth, informational power. The first three are positional: they come with a role and disappear when the role is taken away. A manager can reward, sanction and invoke the right to decide because the organisation has lent her those means. Expert, referent and informational power are personal: they rest on what others believe about your knowledge, your character and the quality of what you know and share.

The distinction matters because the two kinds of power produce different responses. Research on influence, summarised by Yukl (2020), consistently links personal power with commitment, while positional power — and coercive power above all — tends to produce compliance at best and resistance at worst. Compliance gets the task done while you are watching. Commitment gets it done when you are not in the room, which in hybrid organisations is most of the time. In MASCO101 you met French and Raven as a negotiation lens. Here the question is longer-term: which bases can you build deliberately, and how long do they take to earn?

Credibility is judged on impact over time

Personal power is not a feeling you have about yourself. It exists only in other people’s judgement of you, and they form that judgement slowly and from evidence. Stephen M.R. Covey’s Four Cores of Credibility describe what that evidence is about: integrity (are you honest and consistent?), intent (whose interest are you serving?), capabilities (can you do it?) and results (have you done it before?). The first two concern character, the second two competence. A newcomer can often show capability quickly. Integrity and intent take longer, because people can only infer them from behaviour repeated across situations.

This is where the gap between intention and impact opens. You judge yourself by your intentions in the moment: you meant to be efficient, not dismissive; you meant to be thorough, not controlling. Others judge you by the impact they experience, and they remember the pattern. Kouzes and Posner, after decades of collecting leaders’ personal-best cases, reduce credibility to one test: do what you say you will do. Consistency over time is not a soft virtue here. It is the mechanism by which referent power is built, and the reason why a single broken promise costs more than a single kept one earns.

The same logic explains executive presence, which you explored in MASCO101 as gravitas, communication and appearance (Hewlett, 2014). Presence that is not backed by a track record reads as performance. Presence that rests on a record reads as confidence.

Ethos is built before you speak

More than two thousand years before French and Raven, Aristotle analysed the same problem from the speaker’s side. In the Rhetoric he identified three ‘proofs’ that make an audience accept a message. Ethos is the credibility of the speaker’s character, pathos the emotional state the speaker creates in the audience, and logos the reasoning of the argument itself. Aristotle insisted that ethos must be shown in the speech, but in organisations it is mostly formed long before it: by the meetings you prepared for, the colleague you credited, the deadline you kept.

The triangle is a useful diagnostic for anyone who must persuade without authority. Analysts tend to over-invest in logos — more data, a sharper model — and are surprised when sceptical colleagues are not moved. Charismatic speakers lean on pathos and lose the room once the questions start. A message persuades when it answers three questions at once: why should they believe you, why should they care now, and why does the argument hold? Distilling your message to one sentence, as Bostelaar recommends, forces you to decide which of the three your audience doubts most.

Generative AI adds a new twist. It makes logos cheap: anyone can produce a polished argument in seconds, and AI-mediated messages can make senders look better than they are, as Walther’s hyperpersonal model predicted for online communication decades ago. When polish is abundant, it stops signalling competence. And as synthetic voices and videos spread, Chesney and Citron warn of a ‘liar’s dividend’: genuine evidence can be dismissed as fake. Both trends push credibility back to the oldest source Aristotle named — the observable, consistent character of the person speaking. Use AI to rehearse your argument and to find its weakest point. Do not let it speak for you.

Your title lends you power for as long as you hold the role; your credibility is the only authority people will follow when the title is not enough.
