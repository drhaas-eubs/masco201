Stakeholders are a leadership problem, not a list

Freeman (1984) defined a stakeholder as any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of an organisation's objectives. The definition is deliberately broad. It includes employees, customers, suppliers and financiers, but also communities, regulators and activist groups. Freeman's central claim was managerial rather than moral: organisations that ignore these relationships will be surprised by them. Later work (Freeman, Harrison and Wicks, 2007) sharpened the point — value is created jointly with stakeholders, and their interests overlap more often than conventional trade-off thinking assumes.

The difficulty for leaders is not identifying stakeholders but deciding where to spend limited attention. Most students arrive from MASCO101 knowing the power-interest grid, a useful first sort. Mitchell, Agle and Wood (1997) offer a more precise lens. They argue that stakeholders become salient to managers through three attributes: power (the ability to impose their will), legitimacy (a socially accepted claim) and urgency (a claim that is time-sensitive and important to the stakeholder). A stakeholder with only one attribute is latent; with two, expectant; with all three, definitive.

Two consequences matter for leaders. First, salience is dynamic. A works council with legitimacy and power becomes definitive the moment its concern becomes urgent. Second, some combinations are dangerous: a stakeholder with power and urgency but without recognised legitimacy may act outside formal channels. Mapping salience is therefore not a one-off exercise for the project charter but a routine leadership habit.

What distance does to salience

Hybrid and remote work have changed the conditions under which leaders read stakeholders. In an office or a plant, urgency is often visible: tension in a meeting, a delegation at the door, rumours in the canteen. Through a screen, these signals arrive late or not at all. The result is a predictable error: leaders give attention to the stakeholders who are easiest to reach, not to those who are most salient.

Lojeski and Reilly (2008) describe this as virtual distance. It has three components. Physical distance covers geography and time zones. Operational distance covers daily friction: technical problems, large teams, multitasking during calls. Affinity distance covers the deeper sense of shared values, relationships and mutual dependence. Their research suggests that affinity distance has the strongest effect on trust, innovation and performance — and it is the easiest to overlook because it does not show up in a project plan.

Intercultural differences, covered in the Boostcamp episode and in the MASCO101 Culture Map, add to this. Signals of disagreement that are explicit in one culture may be subtle in another, and the subtle ones are the first to disappear in a video call. A leader who relies on monthly all-hands calls and written FAQs may believe that silence means agreement, when it means distance.

Choosing the channel is a leadership decision

Daft and Lengel (1986) argued that communication media differ in richness: their capacity for immediate feedback, multiple cues, natural language and personal focus. Face-to-face conversation is richest; written reports are leanest. Effective managers match richer media to ambiguous, emotional or high-stakes messages, and leaner media to routine information. Sending a contested change by email is not efficient; it is a signal about how much the leader values the relationship.

Dennis, Fuller and Valacich (2008) refined the idea with media synchronicity theory. Communication consists of conveyance — sharing and processing information — and convergence — agreeing on what it means. Conveyance works well asynchronously, through documents and shared boards that people can study in their own time. Convergence needs synchronous interaction. Good hybrid leaders therefore sequence channels: they send the analysis in advance, then use the live meeting for questions, concerns and commitment rather than for reading slides aloud.

Channel choice also carries symbolic weight. When a leader announces a controversial change in a written update, stakeholders read the channel as a statement that their reaction is not wanted. When she invites the most affected groups to a live conversation first, and only then publishes the written summary, the same content is received very differently. In hybrid settings this sequencing has to be planned deliberately, because the informal corridor conversations that used to repair misunderstandings no longer happen by accident. A useful rule of thumb is that the more salient the stakeholder and the more ambiguous the message, the richer and more synchronous the first contact should be.

Technology and AI: listening at scale, judging in person

Technology can reduce distance when it is used deliberately. AI tools can now cluster comments from surveys, chats and transcripts into themes, which helps leaders notice rising urgency earlier. But two cautions apply. People adopt a new tool only if they see it as useful and easy to use (Davis, 1989), and stakeholders trust AI-supported conclusions only if they can understand how they were reached. Explainable AI research (Gunning et al., 2019) stresses explanations that support appropriate trust, not blind acceptance. Above all, judging legitimacy and responding with respect remain human tasks. AI can tell a leader what stakeholders are saying; it cannot tell her whose claim is fair, or have the difficult conversation for her.

A practical sequence follows from these ideas. First, map salience and re-map whenever a stakeholder's urgency changes. Second, estimate the virtual distance to each salient stakeholder and work on affinity — shared purpose, personal contact, honoured commitments — before operational fixes. Third, design the channel mix: asynchronous conveyance for information, synchronous convergence for meaning and commitment. Fourth, use technology and AI to listen more widely and earlier, but explain to stakeholders how their input is collected and used, and show them what changed as a result.

Map salience often, reduce affinity distance first, and treat every channel choice as a message about how much a stakeholder matters to you.
