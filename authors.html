Unit 1 · Leading in the Contemporary Business Environment

GYGary YuklEvolution of Leadership Theories · Yukl's Hierarchical Taxonomy of Leader Behaviour · Leadership Task × AI Map

Why this thinker

Author of the course's required text. His synthesis of trait, behaviour, power–influence and situational research is the spine against which every other framework in MASCO201 is read.

Intellectual foundation

Industrial and organisational psychologist; long-time professor at the University at Albany, State University of New York. Leadership in Organizations has run through nine editions.

How to use the work

Use the hierarchical taxonomy to diagnose which family of behaviour — task, relations, change or external — a situation demands, and which behaviours a leader habitually neglects.

Limits and critique

The taxonomy is descriptive rather than predictive; behaviour categories overlap in practice, and much of the underlying research relies on survey ratings from Western managerial samples.

Impact

Leadership in Organizations is a standard graduate text worldwide; the taxonomy and his work on influence tactics are widely cited in leadership research.

AZAbraham Zaleznik1924–2011Manager vs. Leader

Why this thinker

Opened the debate on whether managers and leaders are different kinds of people.

Intellectual foundation

Professor at Harvard Business School and a trained psychoanalyst, he brought psychoanalytic thinking into the study of leadership.

How to use the work

Read his 1977 article as the provocation: leaders and managers differ in attitudes to goals, work, relationships and their sense of self.

Limits and critique

Framed the distinction as one between personality types, which later scholars, including Kotter, treated instead as two complementary roles.

Impact

'Managers and leaders: are they different?' (Harvard Business Review, 1977) remains one of the most cited HBR articles on leadership.

JPKJohn P. Kotterb. 1947Manager vs. Leader · Kotter's 8-Step Change Model

Why this thinker

Turned the manager–leader distinction into two complementary systems of action that every organisation needs.

Intellectual foundation

Professor emeritus at Harvard Business School; research on general managers, power and organisational change.

How to use the work

Diagnose whether a unit is over-managed and under-led: is it coping with complexity or with change? Rebalance the time spent on planning and control versus direction and alignment.

Limits and critique

Largely based on case research in large corporations; the dichotomy can be over-simplified in practice, where the two roles blend.

Impact

'What leaders really do' (1990) and Leading Change (1996) are core texts of management education. Note for Unit 8: his 8-Step Change Model is taught there.

BMBBernard M. Bass1925–2007Bass's Four I's of Transformational Leadership

Why this thinker

Made transformational leadership measurable and testable, building on James MacGregor Burns' political concept.

Intellectual foundation

Distinguished professor at Binghamton University (State University of New York); developed, with Bruce Avolio, the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire (MLQ).

How to use the work

Use the four I's to examine how a leader builds trust, communicates a vision, invites challenge and coaches individuals — on top of clear transactional agreements.

Limits and critique

Critics note overlap between the four components, a risk of heroic-leader bias, and that charisma can serve unethical ends; Bass later distinguished authentic from pseudo-transformational leadership.

Impact

The full-range leadership model and the MLQ are among the most researched instruments in leadership studies.

TETyna Eloundou et al.Task-Based AI Exposure

Why this thinker

Provided the first large-scale, task-level estimate of how large language models affect occupations.

Intellectual foundation

Eloundou, Manning and Mishkin (OpenAI) with Daniel Rock (Wharton) rated occupational tasks from the US O*NET database for exposure to LLMs, combining human and model ratings.

How to use the work

Decompose a role into tasks and estimate exposure per task before deciding what to automate, augment or redesign.

Limits and critique

Exposure measures technical potential, not actual adoption, productivity or job loss; the data are US-based, and authors were partly affiliated with an AI developer.

Impact

Published in Science (2024) and widely used in policy and labour-market debates on generative AI.

FDFabrizio Dell'Acqua et al.Jagged Technological Frontier · Centaurs and Cyborgs

Why this thinker

Produced field-experimental evidence on what AI does to the quality of real knowledge work — and where it makes it worse.

Intellectual foundation

A Harvard Business School-led team including Ethan Mollick and Karim Lakhani ran a pre-registered experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants using GPT-4.

How to use the work

Map which tasks lie inside or outside the frontier before delegating; choose between centaur (divide tasks) and cyborg (integrate continuously) modes of working with AI.

Limits and critique

One firm, one model generation and a set of designed tasks; the frontier moves with each model release, so the specific results date quickly.

Impact

The 'jagged frontier' and 'centaurs and cyborgs' have become standard terms in management debate on generative AI.

HMHans Moravecb. 1948Moravec's Paradox

Why this thinker

Explained why the skills humans find effortless are the ones machines find hardest.

Intellectual foundation

Roboticist and long-time researcher at Carnegie Mellon University's Robotics Institute.

How to use the work

Use the paradox to identify which parts of leadership work — social perception, embodied judgement, reading a room — are least likely to be automated.

Limits and critique

Formulated in the 1980s for robotics and symbolic AI; large language models have since automated some tasks once thought hard, so the paradox is a heuristic, not a law.

Impact

Widely cited in AI and future-of-work debates; set out in Mind Children (1988).

Unit 2 · The Leader’s Inner Game: Values, Identity and Authentic Leadership

BGBill Georgeb. 1942True North — Authentic Leadership

Why this thinker

Moved leadership development from imitating great leaders to understanding one's own life story.

Intellectual foundation

Former chair and chief executive of Medtronic; later professor of management practice at Harvard Business School. True North drew on interviews with 125 leaders.

How to use the work

Write your life story, identify your crucibles and define your True North across five dimensions: self-awareness, values, motivations, support team and integrated life.

Limits and critique

Evidence is largely interview-based and focused on successful senior executives; 'authenticity' can be misread as licence to ignore context or others' expectations.

Impact

True North (2007) and Discover Your True North (2015) are central texts of the authentic-leadership movement and widely used in executive education.

WBWarren Bennis1925–2014Crucibles of Leadership

Why this thinker

Showed how defining ordeals shape leaders and why making meaning from them matters more than the event itself.

Intellectual foundation

Founding chair of the Leadership Institute at the University of Southern California and adviser to several US presidents; with Robert J. Thomas he studied leaders aged over 70 and under 35 for Geeks and Geezers (2002).

How to use the work

Identify a crucible, ask what it taught you about yourself and what you do differently since; use this as the basis of the Assessment 2 crucible analysis.

Limits and critique

Retrospective accounts of successful leaders are prone to survivorship and narrative bias.

Impact

Often described as a founder of leadership studies as an academic field; On Becoming a Leader (1989) remains widely read.

RJARobert J. Anderson and William A. AdamsLeadership Circle Profile · AI-Assisted 360 Synthesis

Why this thinker

Built a 360-degree instrument that explains not only what leaders do but the beliefs that drive it.

Intellectual foundation

Anderson founded The Leadership Circle; Adams co-founded The Full Circle Group. The profile integrates adult-development theory, psychology and competency research.

How to use the work

Compare self-ratings with others' ratings on creative competencies and reactive tendencies; locate the reactive habit that limits a strength.

Limits and critique

A proprietary instrument; much of the supporting evidence is published by its developers rather than in independent peer-reviewed studies.

Impact

Set out in Mastering Leadership (2016); used widely in executive coaching and leadership programmes.

SHSShalom H. Schwartzb. 1936Schwartz Theory of Basic Values

Why this thinker

Provided the most widely tested map of human values, showing which values support and which compete with each other.

Intellectual foundation

Social psychologist, professor emeritus at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem; tested the theory in samples from more than 80 countries.

How to use the work

Rank the ten values, locate opposing pairs on the circle and examine which priority wins when they collide under pressure.

Limits and critique

Self-reported values do not always predict behaviour; later refinements split the ten values into nineteen, which complicates the original structure.

Impact

Basis of the human-values module of the European Social Survey and one of the most cited theories in cross-cultural psychology.

GGGraham GibbsGibbs' Reflective Cycle with AI

Why this thinker

Gave learners a simple structure for turning experience into learning.

Intellectual foundation

Educational developer; the cycle appeared in Learning by Doing (1988), published by the Further Education Unit at Oxford Polytechnic.

How to use the work

Move through description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion and action plan for one experience at a time.

Limits and critique

Can become a mechanical checklist; it gives little guidance on challenging one's own assumptions.

Impact

One of the most widely used reflective models in education, nursing and professional development.

MSMrinank Sharma et al.Sycophancy Check

Why this thinker

Documented that AI assistants trained with human feedback tend to tell users what they appear to want to hear.

Intellectual foundation

Researchers at Anthropic and collaborating universities tested several AI assistants across free-form tasks and analysed human preference data.

How to use the work

Before trusting agreeable AI feedback, ask for the strongest opposing case without revealing your own view.

Limits and critique

Results describe particular models at a point in time; the size of the effect changes with training methods.

Impact

Presented at ICLR 2024 and widely cited in research on the reliability of AI assistants.

Unit 3 · Leadership Self-Diagnosis: Emotional Intelligence & Psychological Safety

DGDaniel Golemanb. 1946Goleman's Five Components of Emotional Intelligence · Six Leadership Styles · AI Style-Spotting Protocol (adapted)

Why this thinker

Made emotional intelligence a mainstream leadership competency and translated it into six observable leadership styles that students can diagnose in themselves.

Intellectual foundation

Psychologist with a PhD from Harvard University; long-time science journalist at The New York Times; his book Emotional Intelligence (1995) built on the academic concept introduced by Salovey and Mayer (1990). Co-founder of the Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning (CASEL).

How to use the work

Use the five components as a self-scan, and the six styles as a repertoire: identify your dominant, back-up and missing styles, then practise switching deliberately to the style the situation needs.

Limits and critique

Critics argue that his broad, mixed model of emotional intelligence overlaps with personality traits and that some performance claims rest on proprietary consultancy data rather than peer-reviewed studies.

Impact

'Leadership that gets results' (2000) is one of the most widely used Harvard Business Review articles in leadership teaching, and Primal Leadership (2002, with Boyatzis and McKee) carried the ideas into executive development.

ACEAmy C. EdmondsonThe Leader's Toolkit for Psychological Safety · Implicit Voice Theories

Why this thinker

Showed that whether people speak up depends on the climate leaders create, and gave leaders concrete behaviours to build it.

Intellectual foundation

Novartis Professor of Leadership and Management at Harvard Business School; PhD in organisational behaviour from Harvard; earlier worked as an engineer with Buckminster Fuller. Her 1999 Administrative Science Quarterly paper defined team psychological safety.

How to use the work

Apply the three leader moves — set the stage, invite participation, respond productively — and audit your own reactions to bad news and dissent.

Limits and critique

Psychological safety is easily misread as comfort or niceness; Edmondson herself stresses it must be combined with high standards. Most evidence is correlational and drawn from team-level studies.

Impact

Her work became a reference point for organisations studying what makes teams effective, and The Fearless Organization (2018) is widely used in management education. She is repeatedly ranked in the Thinkers50.

JRDJames R. DetertImplicit Voice Theories

Why this thinker

Explained why employees stay silent even under leaders who invite input: they carry learned rules about the risks of speaking up.

Intellectual foundation

Professor at the University of Virginia's Darden School of Business, with a joint appointment at the Batten School; with Amy Edmondson he identified implicit voice theories through interviews and surveys in a multinational company (Academy of Management Journal, 2011).

How to use the work

Name the rules that operate in your team, test them openly and disprove them through repeated, visible responses to people who speak up.

Limits and critique

The theories describe self-censorship well but give leaders limited guidance on how quickly deeply learned rules can be unlearned.

Impact

Shifted research on employee voice from individual courage alone to the beliefs that organisations and leaders create; his book Choosing Courage (2021) carries the work to practitioners.

RWPRosalind W. Picardb. 1962Affective Computing

Why this thinker

Founded the field that asks whether machines can recognise and respond to emotion — the technology behind today's sentiment and emotion tools.

Intellectual foundation

Professor at the MIT Media Lab, where she founded the Affective Computing research group; her book Affective Computing (1997) defined the field. Co-founder of the companies Affectiva and Empatica.

How to use the work

Use her framing to separate signal, inference and response, and to ask what a tool actually measures before letting it inform a judgement about people.

Limits and critique

Emotion-recognition accuracy is contested, especially across cultures and contexts; workplace use raises consent and surveillance concerns now restricted by the EU AI Act.

Impact

Affective computing is an established research area with applications in health and wearables, and the field is central to current debates on emotion AI regulation.

CNClifford Nass1958–2013Computers Are Social Actors (CASA)

Why this thinker

Demonstrated that people respond to computers as if they were social partners, which explains why AI feedback can feel personal and persuasive.

Intellectual foundation

Professor of communication at Stanford University; with Byron Reeves he wrote The Media Equation (1996), and with Youngme Moon he summarised the CASA experiments in the Journal of Social Issues (2000).

How to use the work

Treat the warmth and politeness of an AI coach as a design effect, not evidence of understanding, and decide deliberately how much weight its feedback deserves.

Limits and critique

Many CASA experiments used simple desktop computers and student samples; how far the effects hold for today's conversational AI is still being tested.

Impact

CASA remains a foundational paradigm in human-computer interaction and is widely cited in research on chatbots and voice assistants.

Unit 4 · Navigating Challenging Dilemmas: From Technical Fixes to Adaptive Leadership

RAHRonald A. Heifetzb. 1951Technical vs. Adaptive Challenges · Balcony and Dance Floor

Why this thinker

Gave leaders the single most useful diagnostic for dilemmas: is this a problem an expert can fix, or one that requires people to change?

Intellectual foundation

Trained as a psychiatrist (MD, Harvard Medical School) and as a cellist; founding director of the Center for Public Leadership at Harvard Kennedy School. Developed adaptive leadership in Leadership Without Easy Answers (1994) and, with Marty Linsky, Leadership on the Line (2002) and The Practice of Adaptive Leadership (2009, with Alexander Grashow). Co-founder of Cambridge Leadership Associates.

How to use the work

Diagnose the technical and adaptive parts of a problem, regulate the heat, give the work back to the people who must change, and move regularly between the dance floor and the balcony.

Limits and critique

The distinction is conceptual rather than measurable, and empirical tests of adaptive leadership remain limited; in practice the line between technical and adaptive is often contested.

Impact

Adaptive leadership is a core framework in leadership education at Harvard Kennedy School and in executive programmes worldwide, and it anchors the crucible analysis in Assessment 2.

DSDave Snowdenb. 1954Cynefin for Leaders · Cynefin × AI Fit (adapted)

Why this thinker

Showed leaders that the right way to decide depends on the nature of the context, not on a universal method.

Intellectual foundation

Welsh management consultant and researcher; developed the Cynefin framework while working in knowledge management at IBM around 1999 and later founded Cognitive Edge, now The Cynefin Company. 'Cynefin' is a Welsh word often translated as 'habitat' or 'place of multiple belongings'.

How to use the work

Identify the domain you are in, use the matching decision mode — categorise, analyse, probe or act — and watch for the moment a crisis moves from one domain to another.

Limits and critique

Assigning a situation to a domain is itself a judgement that can be wrong; the framework is sometimes simplified into a static 2×2 matrix, which Snowden himself rejects. The 'simple' domain was later renamed 'obvious' and then 'clear'.

Impact

The 2007 Harvard Business Review article with Mary Boone won the Academy of Management OB Division's Outstanding Practitioner-Oriented Publication Award (2008) and made Cynefin a standard tool in management, public policy and agile practice.

WTCW. Timothy CoombsSituational Crisis Communication Theory

Why this thinker

Turned crisis communication from intuition into evidence: the response must match the responsibility stakeholders attribute to the organisation.

Intellectual foundation

Professor of communication at Texas A&M University; developed SCCT from attribution theory through a series of experimental studies, summarised in Corporate Reputation Review (2007) and in his book Ongoing Crisis Communication.

How to use the work

Classify the crisis by attributed responsibility, check crisis history and prior reputation, give protective information first, then choose deny, diminish or rebuild strategies that match the threat.

Limits and critique

Much of the evidence comes from experiments with hypothetical scenarios and student participants; social media and multi-stakeholder crises are harder to classify.

Impact

SCCT is one of the most widely applied theories in crisis communication research and practice.

RPRaja Parasuraman1950–2015Levels of Automation

Why this thinker

Showed that the question is not whether to automate, but which stage of a decision to automate and to what degree.

Intellectual foundation

Professor of psychology at George Mason University and a pioneer of neuroergonomics; with Thomas Sheridan and Christopher Wickens he published the stages-and-levels model of human interaction with automation (2000), building on Sheridan's earlier scale of automation levels.

How to use the work

Map each decision onto the four stages, set the level of automation deliberately for each, and keep high-stakes decision selection under strong human control.

Limits and critique

The model was developed for engineered systems such as aviation and process control; applying it to generative AI and organisational decisions requires adaptation.

Impact

The model is a standard reference in human factors engineering and in current debates on human oversight of AI.

BJDBerkeley J. DietvorstAlgorithm Aversion and Appreciation

Why this thinker

Named algorithm aversion: people abandon an algorithm after seeing it err, even when it outperforms human judgement.

Intellectual foundation

Behavioural scientist at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business; with Joseph Simmons and Cade Massey he published the experiments defining algorithm aversion (Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2015).

How to use the work

Expect teams to over-react to visible algorithm errors, and set trust by systematic track record rather than memorable failures.

Limits and critique

The effect varies with task, expertise and whether people can adjust the algorithm's output; later studies found contrary tendencies.

Impact

Algorithm aversion has become a central concept in research on human-AI decision-making.

JMLJennifer M. LoggAlgorithm Aversion and Appreciation

Why this thinker

Showed the opposite tendency: lay people often prefer algorithmic advice to human advice.

Intellectual foundation

Researcher at Georgetown University's McDonough School of Business; with Julia Minson and Don Moore she published the algorithm appreciation studies (Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2019).

How to use the work

Check whether a team's trust in AI reflects evidence or simply deference to a machine, and note that experts tend to discount algorithmic advice.

Limits and critique

Findings come largely from estimation and forecasting tasks; preferences may differ for moral or interpersonal decisions.

Impact

Together with algorithm aversion, the work frames the leader's task as calibrating trust in AI rather than maximising or minimising it.

GKGary Kleinb. 1944AI Red-Team Pre-Mortem (adapted)

Why this thinker

Showed how experts actually decide under pressure and gave teams the pre-mortem to overcome overconfidence in their plans.

Intellectual foundation

Research psychologist and pioneer of naturalistic decision making; developed the Recognition-Primed Decision model from field studies of firefighters and military commanders, described in Sources of Power (1998). Published the pre-mortem technique in Harvard Business Review (2007).

How to use the work

Before committing to a plan, assume it has failed, have everyone write down reasons independently, then address the most plausible ones.

Limits and critique

The pre-mortem depends on genuine psychological safety; in hierarchical teams people may still hold back the most uncomfortable causes.

Impact

The pre-mortem is widely used in project management, risk management and strategy, and was popularised further by Daniel Kahneman.

Unit 5 · Moral Courage & Ethical Leadership Under Pressure

JRRJames R. Rest1941–1999Rest's Four-Component Model

Why this thinker

Explained why knowing what is right is not enough: ethical action can fail at four distinct points, which makes the model a diagnostic for leaders under pressure.

Intellectual foundation

Moral psychologist at the University of Minnesota, working in the cognitive-developmental tradition of Lawrence Kohlberg; developer of the Defining Issues Test (DIT) for measuring moral judgement.

How to use the work

Diagnose a situation component by component — sensitivity, judgement, motivation, character — and design interventions for the component most likely to fail.

Limits and critique

The components interact rather than run strictly in sequence; the model says little about organisational context, and much of the measurement work focused on moral judgement only.

Impact

The four-component model became a standard framework in business-ethics and professional-ethics education, including medicine, accounting and management.

RMKRushworth M. Kidder1944–2012Kidder's Right-vs-Right Paradigms · Kidder's Moral Courage Model

Why this thinker

Gave leaders a practical vocabulary for the hardest dilemmas — right versus right — and treated moral courage as something that can be learned.

Intellectual foundation

Former columnist and senior editor at The Christian Science Monitor; founded the Institute for Global Ethics in 1990.

How to use the work

Name the paradigm (truth vs. loyalty, individual vs. community, short vs. long term, justice vs. mercy), apply ends-based, rule-based and care-based reasoning, and check principles, danger and endurance before acting.

Limits and critique

Written for a general audience rather than as empirical research; the paradigms simplify complex situations and the resolution principles can point in different directions without ranking them.

Impact

How Good People Make Tough Choices (1995) and Moral Courage (2005) are widely used in leadership and ethics programmes in business, education and the public sector.

MCGMary C. GentileGiving Voice to Values

Why this thinker

Shifted values-driven leadership from 'what is right?' to 'how do I act on it effectively?', which is the gap where most professionals actually struggle.

Intellectual foundation

Business-ethics educator; Giving Voice to Values was launched with the Aspen Institute and Yale School of Management as founding partners, later housed at Babson College and then at the University of Virginia's Darden School of Business.

How to use the work

Assume you know what is right, anticipate reasons and rationalisations, script the conversation, rehearse it with peers and coach one another.

Limits and critique

Focuses on situations where the right course is relatively clear; less guidance for genuine right-vs-right dilemmas, and evidence of long-term behavioural effects is still developing.

Impact

The curriculum has been adopted by business schools and companies worldwide and is freely available as teaching material.

AMAndreas MatthiasThe Responsibility Gap

Why this thinker

Named the problem leaders face when learning systems act in ways nobody fully controls: harm without a clearly responsible agent.

Intellectual foundation

Philosopher of technology; the argument rests on the classic conditions for moral responsibility — control and knowledge — applied to machine learning.

How to use the work

Before deployment, ask who controls and who can foresee the system's behaviour, and assign named human responsibility where those conditions are weak.

Limits and critique

Critics argue the gap can be closed by institutional and legal design, and that responsibility can be shared rather than lost.

Impact

The 2004 paper is one of the most cited texts in AI ethics and shapes current debates on accountability and human oversight.

MCEMadeleine Clare ElishMoral Crumple Zone

Why this thinker

Showed empirically that blame for failures of automated systems tends to land on the nearest human operator — a warning for leaders who deploy AI.

Intellectual foundation

Anthropologist of technology; developed the concept through historical cases of automation in aviation and nuclear power, first as a Data & Society working paper.

How to use the work

In any human-in-the-loop design, check whether the human has real control, or only absorbs the blame, and redesign authority, information and training accordingly.

Limits and critique

Built from qualitative case analysis; it identifies a pattern rather than measuring how often it occurs.

Impact

The term 'moral crumple zone' is widely used in research and policy on human oversight of AI, including debates on autonomous vehicles.

Unit 6 · Leading With and Through Stakeholders in Hybrid and Remote Environments

REFR. Edward Freemanb. 1951Stakeholder Theory

Why this thinker

Made stakeholder relationships a core task of strategic management rather than a matter of public relations or philanthropy.

Intellectual foundation

Philosopher and management scholar; professor at the University of Virginia's Darden School of Business. Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach (1984) is the founding text of the field.

How to use the work

Identify everyone who affects or is affected by the organisation's objectives, look for ways to create value for several stakeholders at once, and manage the relationships continuously.

Limits and critique

Critics argue that 'serving all stakeholders' gives managers no clear objective function and can dilute accountability; prioritisation needs additional tools.

Impact

Stakeholder thinking now underpins corporate governance debates, CSR and ESG reporting and the 'stakeholder capitalism' discussion.

RKMRonald K. Mitchell, Bradley R. Agle and Donna J. WoodStakeholder Salience Model · AI Stakeholder Listening

Why this thinker

Answered the practical question Freeman's broad definition left open: which stakeholders really count for managers, and when.

Intellectual foundation

Management scholars whose 1997 Academy of Management Review article combined stakeholder theory with theories of power, legitimacy and urgency.

How to use the work

Score each stakeholder on power, legitimacy and urgency, classify them as latent, expectant or definitive, and re-map as attributes change.

Limits and critique

The attributes are perceived and subjective; scoring can be manipulated, and the model describes managerial attention rather than what is ethically right.

Impact

One of the most cited articles in stakeholder research; the salience typology is standard in project, strategy and CSR teaching.

RLDRichard L. Daft and Robert H. LengelMedia Richness Theory

Why this thinker

Explained why the choice of communication channel affects whether ambiguous messages are understood — a central issue in hybrid leadership.

Intellectual foundation

Organisation theorists; their 1986 Management Science article linked information-processing theory to the choice of communication media.

How to use the work

Classify the message by ambiguity and stakes, and choose a rich channel for equivocal or emotional content, a lean channel for routine information.

Limits and critique

Empirical support is mixed for newer media; people learn to use lean channels richly, and the theory underplays social and cultural influences on media choice.

Impact

A foundational theory in organisational communication and information systems, still widely used to discuss virtual and hybrid work.

KSLKaren Sobel LojeskiVirtual Distance

Why this thinker

Showed that the real cost of virtual work lies in perceived distance — especially affinity distance — rather than in geography alone.

Intellectual foundation

Researcher and consultant on virtual work; developed the Virtual Distance model with Richard R. Reilly in Uniting the Virtual Workforce (2008).

How to use the work

Assess physical, operational and affinity distance for a team or stakeholder relationship, then act first on the affinity component.

Limits and critique

Much of the evidence comes from proprietary consulting data; the components overlap and are hard to measure independently.

Impact

Widely cited in practitioner and academic discussions of remote and hybrid leadership.

ARDAlan R. DennisMedia Synchronicity Theory

Why this thinker

Refined media choice by separating conveying information from converging on meaning — a practical guide for asynchronous and live work.

Intellectual foundation

Information-systems scholar at Indiana University's Kelley School of Business; developed media synchronicity theory with Robert M. Fuller and Joseph S. Valacich.

How to use the work

Split a communication process into conveyance and convergence, use asynchronous channels for the first and synchronous ones for the second.

Limits and critique

The theory is complex to operationalise and has been tested mainly in group-task settings.

Impact

A leading theory for designing distributed collaboration and choosing between synchronous and asynchronous tools.

FDDFred D. DavisTechnology Acceptance Model

Why this thinker

Explained, with two simple perceptions, why people adopt or resist new technology — essential when leaders introduce tools to stakeholders.

Intellectual foundation

Information-systems scholar; developed the Technology Acceptance Model in his doctoral work at MIT Sloan and published it in MIS Quarterly in 1989.

How to use the work

Before rollout, test whether stakeholders see the tool as useful and easy to use, and address both perceptions explicitly.

Limits and critique

Criticised as too parsimonious: it neglects social influence, organisational context and emotion, which later extensions such as UTAUT added.

Impact

One of the most cited models in information-systems research and a reference point for studies of AI adoption.

DGDavid GunningExplainable AI for Stakeholders

Why this thinker

Framed explainability as a condition for appropriate human trust in AI, which leaders need when AI outputs affect stakeholders.

Intellectual foundation

Programme manager of DARPA's Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) programme; lead author of the 2019 Science Robotics overview.

How to use the work

Require that AI outputs come with explanations suited to their audience, including confidence levels and known limits.

Limits and critique

Explanations can be simplified to the point of misleading, and explainability does not by itself guarantee fairness or accuracy.

Impact

The XAI programme helped establish explainable AI as a research field and influenced regulatory expectations of transparency.

Unit 7 · Earned Authority: Leadership Presence and Credibility Without a Title

JRFJohn R.P. French1913–1995French & Raven's Power Bases — Leader Lens

Why this thinker

Co-author of the most widely cited typology of social power, still the starting point for analysing influence without formal authority.

Intellectual foundation

Social psychologist at the University of Michigan’s Institute for Social Research, working in the group-dynamics tradition founded by Kurt Lewin.

How to use the work

Audit which power bases a leader holds and which a situation requires; distinguish compliance from commitment.

Limits and critique

The typology describes sources of power but says little about how they are built over time or how they combine; the categories overlap in practice.

Impact

The bases of power remain a standard element of leadership and organisational behaviour textbooks, including Yukl.

BHRBertram H. Raven1926–2020French & Raven's Power Bases — Leader Lens

Why this thinker

Extended the original five bases with informational power and developed the power/interaction model of interpersonal influence.

Intellectual foundation

Social psychologist, long-time professor at the University of California, Los Angeles.

How to use the work

Separate the influence of what you know and share (informational power) from your role and your character.

Limits and critique

Later refinements of the model are less widely taught and less tested in organisational settings than the original typology.

Impact

His work made the power bases a standard diagnostic in social psychology, health communication and management.

AAristotle384–322 BCEAristotle's Rhetorical Triangle — Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Why this thinker

The first systematic analysis of persuasion; his three proofs remain the simplest diagnostic of why a message does or does not land.

Intellectual foundation

Greek philosopher, student of Plato and founder of the Lyceum in Athens; the Rhetoric treats persuasion as an art that can be taught.

How to use the work

Check every leadership message for ethos (why believe you?), pathos (why care now?) and logos (why does it hold?).

Limits and critique

Written for public speaking in a small city-state; it does not address mediated, asynchronous or AI-generated communication.

Impact

Foundational to rhetoric, communication studies and modern presentation training across more than two millennia.

Use the LPI as a 360 input and plan development around the least-used practice.

Swedish psychologist; Conradi Eminent Scholar at Florida State University.

Design rehearsal with specific goals, immediate feedback and work at the edge of current ability.
