F17 · Slibrary 5 · Sheet 1/4

Goleman's Five Components of Emotional Intelligence

Five learnable competencies that set outstanding leaders apart

Source (Harvard): Goleman, D. (1998) 'What makes a leader?', Harvard Business Review, 76(6), pp. 93-102.

In one sentenceLeaders stand out less through IQ or expertise than through five learnable emotional capabilities: knowing themselves, controlling their impulses, staying motivated, reading other people and handling relationships well.
About the author(s)Daniel Goleman (b. 1946) is an American psychologist who reported on behavioural science for The New York Times. His book Emotional Intelligence (1995) brought the concept to a mass audience, and he co-founded the Consortium for Research on Emotional Intelligence in Organizations.
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)Lotte Vermeer sees herself as open and fair, yet her team's score on raising concerns fell from 4.1 to 2.9 within a year: which of the five components best explains this gap, and which would she practise first?
F18 · Slibrary 5 · Sheet 2/4

Six Leadership Styles

Six styles, one repertoire — switch as the situation demands

Source (Harvard): Goleman, D. (2000) 'Leadership that gets results', Harvard Business Review, 78(2), pp. 78-90.

In one sentenceLeaders draw on six basic styles — coercive, authoritative, affiliative, democratic, pacesetting and coaching; each affects team climate differently, and the best leaders switch between several as the situation requires.
About the author(s)Daniel Goleman (b. 1946) is an American psychologist and author of Emotional Intelligence (1995). He developed the six styles further with Richard Boyatzis and Annie McKee in Primal Leadership (2002), which linked them to resonant and dissonant leadership.
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)Lotte calls her style coaching, while her team describes overnight rework and 'Who signed this off?' asked in front of everyone: which style do they actually experience, and which two styles should she add before the forecast?
F19 · Slibrary 5 · Sheet 3/4

The Leader's Toolkit for Psychological Safety

Three moves through which one leader makes speaking up safe

Source (Harvard): Edmondson, A.C. (2018) The Fearless Organization: Creating Psychological Safety in the Workplace for Learning, Innovation, and Growth. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley.

In one sentenceWhether people dare to speak up depends largely on three things a leader does: framing the work as uncertain, inviting input with genuine questions, and responding to bad news with appreciation rather than blame.
About the author(s)Amy C. Edmondson is the Novartis Professor of Leadership and Management at Harvard Business School. Her 1999 study introduced team psychological safety into organisational research; her books include Teaming (2012), The Fearless Organization (2018) and Right Kind of Wrong (2023).
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)Harbourline lost about €380,000 because two analysts kept their doubts to themselves: which of the three moves has Lotte neglected, and what exactly will she say in her next team meeting to set the stage?
F20 · Slibrary 5 · Sheet 4/4

Implicit Voice Theories

Unspoken rules that keep people silent with their boss

Source (Harvard): Detert, J.R. and Edmondson, A.C. (2011) 'Implicit voice theories: taken-for-granted rules of self-censorship at work', Academy of Management Journal, 54(3), pp. 461-488.

In one sentencePeople often stay silent with their boss not because of that boss but because of unspoken rules: the boss owns the idea, bring data first, never embarrass the boss, speaking up hurts careers.
About the author(s)James R. Detert is the John L. Colley Professor of Business Administration at the University of Virginia's Darden School of Business and author of Choosing Courage (2021). Amy C. Edmondson is the Novartis Professor of Leadership and Management at Harvard Business School.
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)One Harbourline analyst said: 'We assumed you had already checked it.' Which implicit voice theories operate in Lotte's team, and how could she visibly disconfirm two of them before the next quarterly forecast?

F21 · Slibrary 6 · Sheet 1/4

Affective Computing

Machines that read emotion — and the assumption behind each step

Source (Harvard): Picard, R.W. (1997) Affective Computing. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.

In one sentenceAffective computing builds systems that detect and respond to human emotion; for leaders, the key question is whether a signal read from a face, a voice or a text really shows what someone feels.
About the author(s)Rosalind W. Picard is Professor of Media Arts and Sciences at the MIT Media Lab, where she founded the Affective Computing research group. She co-founded the companies Affectiva and Empatica, which develop emotion-analytics and wearable health technology.
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)The operations director wants an AI assistant that flags 'negative sentiment' in Harbourline's meetings: what signal would it measure, what would it infer, and what would it tell Lotte about why her analysts stay silent?
F22 · Slibrary 6 · Sheet 2/4

EU AI Act — Workplace Emotion-Recognition Ban

Emotion-reading AI at work is prohibited — with narrow exceptions

Source (Harvard): European Parliament and Council of the European Union (2024) Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act). Official Journal of the European Union, L, 12 July 2024.

In one sentenceSince February 2025, EU law forbids using AI to infer the emotions of employees or students at work or in education, unless the system serves medical or safety purposes.
About the author(s)The regulation was proposed by the European Commission in April 2021 and adopted by the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union in 2024. It is the first comprehensive horizontal law on artificial intelligence and applies in stages between 2025 and 2027.
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)Harbourline's proposed meeting assistant would flag 'negative sentiment' in analysts' voices at the Rotterdam office: would deploying it fall under the Article 5(1)(f) ban, and what should Lotte tell the operations director?
F23 · Slibrary 6 · Sheet 3/4

Computers Are Social Actors (CASA)

Minimal human cues trigger full social responses to machines

Source (Harvard): Nass, C. and Moon, Y. (2000) 'Machines and mindlessness: social responses to computers', Journal of Social Issues, 56(1), pp. 81-103.

In one sentencePeople automatically treat computers that show even minimal human cues — a voice, a name, turn-taking — like social partners: they are polite, they reciprocate and they believe flattery, even when they know it is a machine.
About the author(s)Clifford Nass (1958–2013) was Professor of Communication at Stanford University, and Byron Reeves is Professor of Communication at Stanford. Youngme Moon, co-author of the 2000 paper, is Professor of Business Administration at Harvard Business School.
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)If Lotte asked an AI coach to assess her leadership, why might its friendly verdict feel more convincing than her team's pulse score of 2.9 — and how would she test the AI's verdict against evidence?
F24 · Slibrary 6 · Sheet 4/4

AI Style-Spotting Protocol

The AI's style label is a hypothesis, never the answer

Source (Harvard): Goleman, D. (2000) 'Leadership that gets results', Harvard Business Review, 78(2), pp. 78-90.

In one sentenceAn AI tool suggests which of Goleman's six styles an anonymised leadership episode shows; students then test that label against the evidence, overrule it where needed and declare what the AI contributed.
About the author(s)This is the course adaptation by Dr. Hildegard Haas. It builds on Daniel Goleman (b. 1946), the American psychologist whose 2000 Harvard Business Review article 'Leadership that gets results' defined the six styles from Hay/McBer research on executives.
Guiding question (Harbourline Analytics case)Feed the three anonymous Harbourline interview statements about Lotte into an AI tool: which style does it propose, which quotation would confirm or overrule its label, and how would you declare its contribution?
